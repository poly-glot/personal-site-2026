Ten years ago I described myself as "18/24", by which I meant always on and
always available. I wanted to be involved in everything, and I wore that as a
badge of honour, because being needed felt like proof that I cared about the
work.

Growing teams changed my mind. I grew a development studio from one person to
eight remote developers, and over the past decade I have helped small
engineering groups become mid-sized teams (the longer version is on my [About
page](/about/)). Somewhere along the way I stopped judging my work by how much I
did myself, and started judging it by how much the teams around me could do,
especially on the days I wasn't there. Those teams now include AI agents that
write code and run tasks, so the same judgement applies to them. That is one of
the first things I would look for in a Principal Engineer, next to the technical
judgement the role has always needed.

## From always on to guardrails {#guardrails}

When I was always on, every outage, question and decision came to me.

</Wide>

On the left, the problems queue up for me. The team can only go as fast as I
answer, and nothing moves while I sleep. On the right, the same problems meet a
guardrail first, whether the one working on them is a person or an AI agent. The
familiar ones are handled there, and only the new or hard ones reach me, which
leaves me time to set the target the guardrails aim at.

A guardrail is anything that catches a problem early, or tells the next
intelligence, real or artificial, what to do, without waiting for someone
senior. Four kinds cover most of what I mean:

- A check stops a change, whether a person or an AI agent wrote it, that breaks
  something other teams rely on, such as an API contract or a security rule,
  before it ships.
- A default makes the right way the easy way, such as a service template that
  already has logging, timeouts and limits set up, so a person or an AI agent
  starting a new service gets them without asking.
- A runbook is a page of steps for a failure that will happen again, so whoever
  or whatever is on call, a person or an AI agent, can fix it.
- A decision record is a short note of a past decision and why it was made, so
  the next similar decision, made by a person or an AI agent, doesn't need the
  person who made the first.

Here is a small one of mine. If I forgot to fill in a secret, the repository
that sets up my own cloud services used a blank one, and one of my apps would
have signed its output with a blank key. I [added a
check](https://github.com/poly-glot/firebase-cloud/commit/76ab5f96a2b48f03e1eda7d25d7fb0c46f8ce62e),
so the deploy now stops before it changes anything, with a message that names
the secret to fill in. The next one to hit it, whether me in six months or an AI
agent deploying for me, doesn't have to remember anything.

That check works like a thermostat, and so does any good guardrail. You set the
target temperature. It measures the room, compares the reading with the target
and switches the heating on or off. Nobody has to stand in the room and feel the
cold.

## The other half of the job {#the-role}

A thermostat still needs someone to choose the temperature, and that is the
other half of a Principal Engineer's job. Titles vary between companies, but a
Principal Engineer is usually one of the most senior engineers in an
organisation, and leads without managing anyone. Amazon calls its principal
engineers its [most senior individual
contributors](https://amazon.jobs/content/en/teams/principal-engineering/tenets),
and the first of their tenets is "Exemplary practitioner". It describes its
principal engineering community as [visionary leaders and hands-on
builders](https://amazon.jobs/content/en/teams/principal-engineering) working on
its hardest problems. Dropbox's [public career
framework](https://dropbox.github.io/dbx-career-framework/ic6_principal_software_engineer.html)
asks a Principal Engineer to deliver results against a company-level business
objective, to own goals that span several teams and several years, and to make
technical choices that have no one clearly correct answer, then rally the
organisation behind them with a clear rationale.

So the job has two parts that need each other. One is setting the target with
engineering and product leaders: deciding what good looks like for systems that
several teams depend on, given what the business needs. That takes deep
technical judgement and staying close to the details. The other is building the
guardrails that hold the target, so those teams, and the AI agents working with
them, don't come back for the same answer twice, and the new and hard problems
still reach someone who can solve them. I expect that to matter more as agents
take on more of the work. An agent can only act on a target and guardrails it
can read, so they have to be written down where it can find them.

Will Larson's [guide to what Staff-plus engineers actually
do](https://staffeng.com/guides/what-do-staff-engineers-actually-do/), which
covers Principal Engineers too, covers much of the same ground: setting
technical direction, mentoring and sponsoring other engineers, bringing an
engineering view into decisions, exploring problems the company hasn't solved
yet, and being glue, Tanya Reilly's name for the needed but often invisible work
that keeps a team shipping. Writing a runbook or a decision record is glue.
Reilly's talk on [glue work](https://noidea.dog/glue) argues that it is
technical leadership, but that it often goes unrewarded when people do it before
they are senior. At Principal level it is part of the job, so I would ask for
examples.

## Four questions I'd ask {#what-i-ask}

That is a lot to judge in an interview. At Inspired Thinking Group I streamlined
the technical tests we hire with, and for a Principal Engineer I would want each
question to look at a different part of the job.

- **"Tell me about the largest problem you owned. How many teams did it touch,
  how long did it take, and what changed for the business?"** Scope is what
  separates a Principal Engineer from a strong senior engineer, so I want the
  size of the problem, the time it took and the result, in their own words.
- **"Tell me about a decision your team made that you disagreed with, and lost.
  What did you do next?"** A Principal Engineer's view carries weight, so I want
  to know what they do when it doesn't win. I want to hear that they made their
  case, accepted the result and helped it work, unless the decision was unsafe,
  in which case I want to hear how they escalated it. Someone who agrees in the
  meeting and then keeps fighting the decision while the team builds it is hard
  to work with.
- **"Walk me through a design document, RFC or decision record you wrote that
  changed how teams worked six months later."** Setting the target only helps if
  people can still find the reasoning after the meeting, so this tells me
  whether the candidate's writing gets used. A record that only sits in a folder
  hasn't done its job, and I [wrote about that](/blog/adr-as-build-gates/)
  separately.
- **"Where does your influence stop, and how do you know?"** A Principal Engineer
  leads people who don't report to them, so their influence always runs out
  somewhere: at another team's priorities, at a budget, or at a leader who sees
  it differently. I want to hear where theirs ran out and what they did next.

## A real document instead of a whiteboard {#a-real-document}

Questions tell me how someone talks about their work. To see their technical
judgement, I would want to watch them work on something. The usual choice is a
whiteboard system-design interview, in which the candidate sketches a service
such as a photo-sharing site and explains how it would cope with millions of
users. I don't like that format for this role, because it rewards rehearsed
phrases like "put a queue in front of it" and "shard by user" (give each group
of users their own database), whether or not the candidate can find their way
round a real, untidy system.

I would rather hand someone a real design document that several teams depend on,
where the trade-offs have no single right answer, and ask what they would push
back on. A good candidate reads it the way they would read a colleague's: they
compare what it promises with what the systems do, and they ask how each promise
could be checked.

Here is the kind of gap I mean, in a README (the page that explains a project)
that I wrote myself and got wrong. [OpenGuessr](https://openguessr.junaid.guru/)
([Case Study](/work/openguessr/)) is a geography game where players look at a
street somewhere in the world and drop a pin on a map where they think they are.
Its
[README](https://github.com/poly-glot/openguessr/blob/master/README.MD#database-rules-as-the-security-boundary)
says: "Even if a player inspects the database directly, upcoming answers stay
hidden." That sentence has never been true. Every player can read the answers,
which are the map coordinates for all five rounds, as soon as a game is created,
and the one test about hidden answers runs on a sample game whose unrevealed
round has no coordinates, so it passes. I [wrote up what I
found](/blog/openguessr-fair-play/).

Finding a gap like that takes the judgement half of the job, and closing it
takes the guardrail half: here, a test that fails while a player can read an
upcoming answer. I want someone who can do both, and who isn't embarrassed to
say so when they find one, whether the mistake is a colleague's, an AI agent's,
mine or their own.

## Signals I look for {#signals}

Whatever we talked about, I would be listening for a few things that no single
question asks about.

<Checklist>

- They credit other people's work without being asked, because a Principal
  Engineer gets many of their results through other people. If someone says "I"
  about the wins and "we" about the failures, I would ask more.
- They change their mind when they hear something new, and say what changed it.
  They will do the same in a design review.
- They ask about constraints as well as goals. "What can't you change here?"
  tells me as much as "What does success look like?"
- They can name a system they wish they hadn't built. If every story they tell
  ends well, I would ask about the one they left out.
- They can describe what their teams, and any AI agents working with them, do on
  a day they're not there, and what has to wait until they're back.

</Checklist>

## The same test for me {#closing}

I would hold myself to that last signal too. If I went away for a fortnight, a
few things would still wait for me, and each one is a guardrail I haven't built
yet. The 18/24 version of me would have been pleased to be missed. Now I see
each of those things as my unfinished work, whether a person or an AI agent
would do it once the guardrail exists.

You can run the same test this week. Write down what would wait for you if you
were away for a fortnight, pick the item that worries you most, and build the
guardrail for it, even if it is only a runbook that a person or an AI agent
could follow.
