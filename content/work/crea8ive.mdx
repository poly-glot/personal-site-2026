<Chapter label="Strategy" title="Sell logos online to strangers, and deliver every order to the same standard." lede="Every page on that board was written for one reader, the owner of a small business who needed a logo and knew the studio only from its website. That owner was being asked to pay up front for work that had not started yet, from designers they would never meet. The studio&apos;s strategy was about getting that owner to pay, and then delivering what they had paid for, on every order." split>
<Split reverse>
<Steps>
  <Step open name="The aim" hint="a stranger orders, and is satisfied">Winning meant the owner of a small business who had never heard of the studio found the site, chose a package, paid, and ended up with a logo they were happy with. The site offered unlimited revisions and a risk-free 100% satisfaction policy, so an order was finished only when the customer was satisfied.</Step>
  <Step name="Where to play" hint="small businesses anywhere, buying online">The studio sold online to small businesses anywhere in the world. Logos came first, with websites, stationery, brochures and illustration as further packages. The logo portfolio was sorted into 31 industries, from accounting to sport, so an owner could see work done for a business like theirs.</Step>
  <Step name="How to win" hint="a fixed price and every step set out">An owner will pay a stranger up front only if they know the price and what happens next. So every package had a fixed price, in three tiers called Budget, Basic and Advance, starting at $99 for a custom logo. The How it works page set out the eight steps of an order, from signing up to the final files, with the first concepts in two to three days. The cost was that every one of those promises had to be kept on every order.</Step>
  <Step name="What it takes" hint="a team and a site">It took two things, and I built both. The first was a team that could deliver a large number of orders to the same quality, whichever designer drew them. The second was a site that could sell the packages and be found in search on its own.</Step>
  <Step name="How we&apos;d know" hint="projects delivered, customers from search">Success would show in projects delivered and in customers arriving from the search phrases the site was written for. The first has a count. The studio delivered more than 10,000 design projects from 2007 to 2011. Its Resources page shows it also sold through freelance marketplaces such as Elance, so that count covers the whole studio, and the site&apos;s share of it is not known. The second has none, because no figures exist from those years on search rankings or on how many visitors went on to order. So this study can show how the site was built to be found, and cannot say how well it ranked.</Step>
</Steps>

<Chapter label="Content architecture" title="Four templates and two data files drew most of the 52 pages." lede="The site had to sell the packages on its own. Its content architecture, what the site holds and where each page lives, is 52 pages, grouped in the drawing by the job each one does. Most of them were not written one by one. Four templates, page layouts written once and filled in from data, drew the packages, the portfolio, the case studies and the service pages, and two data files held every package and every portfolio piece.">
<ContentMap />
<List>
  <Item title="A readable address for every page.">The site is written in PHP, a language that builds each page on the server when someone asks for it. One PHP file answers every address and picks the page from it, which gave every page a readable address such as /pages/logo_design.</Item>
  <Item title="A new package was a change to the data.">Adding a package, or a logo to the portfolio, meant one more entry in a data file, and the template drew the page.</Item>
</List>

<Chapter label="The craft of 2007" title="Rounded corners made from images, before CSS could draw them." lede="A visitor who arrived from one of those pages then had to trust what they saw, so every page had to look polished. In 2007 that took more work than it does now. Today one line of CSS, the border-radius rule, rounds the corners of a box in any browser. Back then it worked in too few browsers to rely on, so every rounded box on the site is built from images. The middle strip does the work. It is one image repeated down the box, so the box can grow with its text.">
<Crea8iveCorners />
<List>
  <Item title="Pages written to a strict standard.">Every page declares XHTML 1.0 Strict, a version of HTML that follows the stricter rules of XML.</Item>
  <Item title="Patches for Internet Explorer 6.">Internet Explorer 6, Microsoft&apos;s browser of the time, could not show see-through PNG images, so the site patched them for it, and Internet Explorer 6 and 7 each had a stylesheet of their own.</Item>
  <Item title="Four jQuery plugins for the moving parts.">jQuery, a JavaScript library, runs the slideshows (jQuery Cycle), the full-size view of portfolio work (prettyPhoto), the drop-down menus (Superfish) and the tooltips (tipTip).</Item>
</List>

<Chapter label="The limits" title="Where the site falls short of its strategy today." lede="Saving each page as a file let the original code stay online, but it left some links with no page behind them, and other gaps date from 2007. Each one below is set against the part of the strategy it puts at risk. The one a visitor meets first is on the logo portfolio, where the industry filters no longer open.">
<List>
  <Item title="Where to play: the portfolio&apos;s industry filters return a page-not-found error.">The 31 industry filters, the tabs on How it works and the package links, 33 of the 91 internal links I checked, point to addresses that carry a section after the page name. The 2026 deploy saves one file for each page, so those addresses have no file behind them. A fix that saves them too, as 41 more files, is tested on my own computer and is not deployed yet.</Item>
  <Item title="The aim: nobody can order from the archive.">Ordering, My Account and the support tickets ran on a separate system that is not in the archive. The archive sends the order link to the quote page and the log-in link to the contact page, and neither form sends anything. The next step is a line on both pages saying so, so nobody types a brief that goes nowhere.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: one link was broken from the start.">In the original site the link to one case study was mistyped, so it has never opened. The next step is to correct it, and to remove the link to the old support tickets, which have no page in the archive.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: every page has the same title.">All 52 pages share one title, so a search result looked the same whichever case study it led to. Each case study would have stood a better chance in search with a title of its own, named for its phrase, and that is the next step.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: keyword tags stopped counting.">Every page carries a keywords tag and a description tag, lines in the page&apos;s code that visitors never see, aimed at logo searches. Google said in 2009 that it ignores the keywords tag, and a phrase repeated in link after link now reads as stuffing. The next step is to move each phrase into its case study&apos;s title and first heading and drop the keywords tags.</Item>
  <Item title="How to win: on a phone or with a screen reader, ordering is hard.">An owner has to read the packages and fill in forms to order. The site was built for desktop screens and has no viewport tag, the line that tells a phone to fit the page to its screen, so a phone shows the desktop page shrunk. Text contrast is low, form fields and buttons have no names for screen readers, and some links say little more than ‘click here’, though every image has a description. The next step is a viewport tag, names for the form fields and buttons, darker text and link words that say where each link goes.</Item>
</List>
</Chapter>

<Chapter label="The lessons" title="Three things from 2007 I would do the same way today." lede="Those limits are the parts of the site that aged, and each has a next step. Three choices have lasted better. One kept the work even across a team of eight designers, one built most of the pages from four templates, and one kept the site online without rewriting it. I would make each of them the same way today.">
<Split>
<List>
  <Item title="Check the work before the customer sees it.">With eight designers working remotely on different orders, the lead designer&apos;s review of every set of concepts meant each customer met the same standard, whoever drew their logo. I would put the same check into any team that works apart.</Item>
  <Item title="Write a layout once and fill it from data.">Because each kind of page came from one template, every package and every case study followed the same layout, and a change to a template reached every page it drew. The newer sites in my work are built on the same idea, with components and a content model in place of templates.</Item>
  <Item title="Keep an old site as it was.">Moving the code without rewriting it means the site a visitor opens today is the one customers used. Saving each page once means no server has to run the PHP of 2007, and the portfolio filters showed me that saving the pages has to cover every address a link points to.</Item>
</List>
