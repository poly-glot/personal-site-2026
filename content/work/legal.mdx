<Steps>
  <Step open name="The aim" hint="clients get in touch">Winning means a company, a state or a person in a cross-border dispute chooses Ferrow & Lisle and gets in touch. There are two ways in. The 24/7 hotline sits at the top of every page, and a form for a first message is on the home page and on the Contact page, under the firm's promise: ‘Tell us about your matter. A partner will reply within one working day.’</Step>
  <Step name="Where to play" hint="six practice areas, two offices">The firm says it acts ‘for companies, states and individuals when the stakes are high and the facts are contested’. It offers six practice areas, from international arbitration and investigations to tracing and recovering assets. It has offices in London and New York, and it has done client work in Dubai, Singapore, Johannesburg and Mexico City.</Step>
  <Step name="How to win" hint="named people beside the work">The firm's arbitration page says ‘We work as a single team across our two offices, so a client has one point of contact wherever the hearing sits’. So the site names the people. Each practice area page lists specialists, and each professional has a page with a biography and representative matters, such as Eleanor Lisle, the Senior Partner, who leads its international arbitration work. A map on the home page shows where the firm works, with a pin for each office and each city where it has done client work.</Step>
  <Step name="What it takes" hint="staff who keep it current">Practice areas, professionals, offices and news change, and the firm's staff have to keep them current without a developer. The hotline is on every page and the two offices are in every footer, so every page that shows them has to agree. And a client's first message has to reach someone at the firm, and stay private.</Step>
  <Step name="How we'd know" hint="enquiries, and what they ask">Success would show in enquiries. Each one holds the sender's name, email address, subject and message, so over time the firm could see how many clients write in and what they say their matter is about. Calls go straight to the phone, so the firm would count those itself. Ferrow & Lisle is imaginary, so there are no real enquiries and no figures.</Step>

<Chapter label="The limits" title="Where the strategy is not proven yet." lede="Several of these came with the existing theme, which the contract kept as it was. Each one is set against the part of the strategy it puts at risk.">
<List>
  <Item title="The aim: nobody is told when an enquiry arrives.">Nothing in the site&apos;s code sends an email, so a message waits in WordPress until a member of staff opens the list of enquiries. The form says so, but a client who writes in hears nothing from the firm until then.</Item>
  <Item title="The aim: after a message from the home page, the thank-you is out of sight.">A message sent from the form on the home page brings the visitor back to the top of that page, while the thank-you is printed at its foot, out of view, so nothing on screen says the message went. The browser asks for a name and an email address, but the site itself checks for neither, so a message with nothing in it is still saved.</Item>
  <Item title="How to win: every practice area page shows the same three specialists.">Our Specialists lists the first three professionals in the order staff set, so all six practice area pages show Eleanor Lisle, Marcus Ferrow and Priya Raman, and Jonah Whitcombe never appears. Nothing in the content model connects a professional to a practice area, so the list cannot show who works on which.</Item>
  <Item title="How to win: on a desktop, the pins and the professionals show no names.">The map&apos;s six pins carry their names for screen readers, but nothing shows a name on screen, even under the pointer, so a visitor cannot tell which city is which. On the home page the four professionals are portraits with no names until the pointer or the keyboard is on one. Below 768 pixels wide both show their names.</Item>
  <Item title="The aim: a keyboard, or a phone without JavaScript, cannot open every menu.">In the header, the Practice Areas and Media lists open only when a mouse is over them, so a keyboard user cannot reach the pages under them from there, and there is no link to skip past the menu to the content. The form&apos;s name, email and subject fields show only a faint darker border when the keyboard is on them. On a phone with JavaScript switched off the menu button does nothing, although the footer&apos;s menus and the two buttons at the top of the home page still reach the same pages. At 320 pixels wide, the narrowest phone screens, every page also scrolls sideways by 15 pixels.</Item>
  <Item title="The aim: some text is faint, the form has no labels, and the video cannot be paused.">The featured article titles on the home page (3.0 to 1), the dates in the news list (2.3 to 1) and the grey labels in the phone&apos;s list of cities (4.0 to 1) are fainter than the 4.5 to 1 the accessibility guidelines (WCAG) ask for. The form&apos;s four fields are named only by grey text that disappears as you type, and the footer&apos;s links are 20 pixels tall, under the 24 asked for. The home page has seven top-level headings instead of one, and its video loops with no pause button, even when a visitor&apos;s device asks for less motion. The site has not been tested with a screen reader, in Safari or Firefox, or on a real phone.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: a deploy puts back the starting content, and some changes need a developer.">A script called the seed fills the site with its starting content, and the pipeline runs it after every deploy. It puts back every title, order number and field it set, the pictures it attached and every value on the Site settings screen, so a changed hotline number or a seventh pin is lost, and it rebuilds the three menus, so a link staff added is gone. Records staff add are left alone. A portrait can only come from the seed, because a professional&apos;s form has no picture box, and a new pin means working out two numbers from the city&apos;s latitude and longitude. On a live site the seed would run once, at launch.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: nothing checks a change before it goes live, and hardening is unfinished.">No test or check runs in the pipeline, so a change pushed to the main branch goes live without one. The site holds visitors&apos; messages, but it sends no content security policy, a header that tells the browser which scripts to trust, and it still announces its WordPress and PHP versions. Every page loads its two typefaces from Google and WordPress&apos;s own 22.7 KB emoji script, about 16 times the size of the other three scripts together, and no page gives search engines a description of itself.</Item>
  <Item title="How we'd know: there are no real enquiries to count.">Each enquiry is saved with a name, an email address, a subject and a message, but not the page it was sent from, so it would not show which practice area brought a client in. The firm is imaginary and the form asks visitors not to enter real personal information, so there are none to count anyway. The newsletter box and the share buttons on each article do nothing when pressed, and the share counts beside them are numbers typed into the article, so interest in the firm&apos;s writing cannot be counted either.</Item>
</List>

<Chapter label="The lessons" title="Three things I would do the same way again." lede="For the firm, the site had to bring clients to a practice area and its professionals, and on to a call or a message, and stay easy for its staff to keep current. These three choices did most of that work.">
<Split>
<List>
  <Item title="Write down what may change before changing it.">A short written contract said what stays and what goes, as set out further up this study. That is why the layout is still the theme&apos;s while the firm&apos;s name, words and pictures are all new, and it also settled smaller things, such as the formula behind the map. What I would add is keeping the contract current. Later changes to the phone numbers, the video and the portraits never went back into it, so it is out of date in those three places.</Item>
  <Item title="Place the map's pins by a formula.">A pin on the map is placed by two numbers, and the contract fixed the formula that turns a city&apos;s latitude and longitude into them, down to where the pin&apos;s tip sits. I checked all six pins against it, and each tip lands within a pixel of its city. A new office can be placed the same way, by a sum instead of trial and error, though staff still have to do the sum themselves, which is part of one of the limits above.</Item>
  <Item title="Enter each detail once, and let every page read it.">The hotline, the two offices and the six pins are each typed once, on the Site settings screen, and printed from there in every header and footer, on Contact and on the map. A professional works the same way. Changing Eleanor Lisle&apos;s job title on a test copy of the site, run on my own computer, changed it on her home page card and on her own page. Our Specialists prints her name and portrait but no job title, so it stayed as it was.</Item>
