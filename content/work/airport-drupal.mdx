<Chapter label="Strategy" title="Put the answers on the home page, so a traveller arrives ready to fly." lede="For an airport, the site has two jobs. It has to answer what a traveller needs to know on the day they fly, from a delayed flight to the nearest car park, in the order they need it. And it has to stay easy for the airport's own staff to keep those answers right from the first flight of the morning to the last." split>

<Steps>
  <Step open name="The aim" hint="travellers arrive ready">Winning means a traveller turns up ready: at the right terminal, in time for security, with somewhere to park or a train to catch. The airport puts its promise in the line that describes its home page to search engines: ‘Live flights, security wait times and parking at Meridian Airport, Birmingham. Check everything before you leave the house.’ Every page on the site is there to help keep it.</Step>
  <Step name="Where to play" hint="two terminals and a railway station">Meridian is a two-terminal airport in Birmingham, ‘built around a railway station’, with trains from the city centre every 10 minutes. Its four airlines fly mostly short flights to Europe and North Africa. Its travellers include families, people flying for the first time and anyone who needs help from kerb to gate, along with the people dropping them off or meeting them. The site has to work for all of them, on whatever screen they have to hand.</Step>
  <Step name="How to win" hint="the answers, in the order needed">The home page puts the answers in the order a traveller needs them. Travel notices, the airport's warnings such as fog delaying morning departures, sit above everything. The first screen also holds a flight search and the security wait at each terminal. Below them come the departures board, the car parks with their prices, and the way in by train, car or coach, each with a link to a page that has the detail.</Step>
  <Step name="What it takes" hint="staff change them the same morning">The answers change through the day. When a flight is delayed or a lane closes on the approach road, staff have to change the notice, the board and the security waits the same morning, without waiting for a developer or a deploy, a new release of the site's code. And the menu, the footer and the phone number have to agree on every page.</Step>
  <Step name="How we'd know" hint="fewer calls, fewer late travellers">Success would show as fewer calls to the passenger enquiries line, and fewer travellers who miss a gate change or turn up at the wrong terminal. Over time the airport would learn which answers people still had to phone for. Meridian is imaginary, so there are no calls and no figures, and the site runs no analytics, so it does not count visits either.</Step>
</Steps>

<Chapter label="The limits" title="Where the strategy is not proven yet." lede="Nothing on the live site was changed for this study, so each gap is described as it stands. These are the ones a traveller or member of staff would meet first, and the smaller accessibility gaps are named further up, beside the accessibility checks. The one that puts the strategy most at risk is that a deploy puts back what staff change in the page templates. Each limit is set against the part of the strategy it affects.">
<List>
  <Item title="The aim: the flight search ignores what a traveller types.">The flight search at the top of the home page asks for departures or arrivals, a day and a flight or city, then opens Live flights on its departures with every row showing, whatever was chosen. Nothing on Live flights reads what the form sends. The board&apos;s own search box, on Live flights, does filter the rows.</Item>
  <Item title="How to win: on a phone, the board hides whether a flight is delayed.">On a phone the board shows the time, the destination and part of the flight number. The terminal, the gate and the status sit off to the right, in a box that scrolls sideways, with nothing to say they are there. The table is wider than a phone screen, and I gave it no narrower layout. The site has also not been tried on a real phone, with a screen reader, or in Safari or Firefox.</Item>
  <Item title="How to win: the home page shows three different times, and some dates and counts are wrong.">The hero shows the traveller&apos;s real clock. The board starts at 09:42 whatever the hour and says it ‘refreshes every minute’, though nothing refreshes, and the security waits say ‘updated 09:42’. The flight search offers ‘Today, 3 October’, and the hero promises ‘112 destinations’ while the Airlines and destinations page lists 20. A travel notice warns of lane closures ‘until 12 October’, the Shopping page&apos;s offers ended on 9 October, and two of the three closing dates on the Careers page have passed. All of it was typed in as fixed text for a demonstration, and nothing updates it.</Item>
  <Item title="The aim: a mistyped address leaves a traveller with no way back.">An address that does not exist answers with the words ‘Not found’ and nothing else: no header, no menu, no footer and no link to the home page. A traveller who follows an old link has to retype the address. The header, menu and footer come from the page templates in Drupal, and the not-found page I wrote in Next.js draws none of them, only the heading.</Item>
  <Item title="The aim: a few lines on the site do not match what it does.">Four forms say ‘Nothing you type is sent anywhere’, yet sending one would put what was typed into the page&apos;s web address, which reaches the site&apos;s server, although nothing keeps it. The footer&apos;s Instagram, X, YouTube and LinkedIn icons open those networks&apos; real front pages, while the Accessibility page says such links ‘go nowhere in this demonstration’.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: a deploy puts back what staff change in the templates.">Page templates are part of the site&apos;s configuration, which every deploy imports again from the code. So a travel notice, a menu link or the phone number that staff change on the live site goes back to the old version at the next deploy, unless a developer copies the change into the code first. A menu link or the phone number also has to be changed by hand in all 10 templates. Travel notices are what staff would change on a bad morning, which is why this gap matters most.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: pictures and flights are typed in by hand, and some words sit in code.">A member of staff picks a picture by typing its file path, widths and sizes into text boxes, with no upload and no preview, so a typing mistake shows a broken picture, and a new picture needs a developer to add the file first. Each flight row is ten settings typed by hand, because there is no flight feed. Some words and numbers are written into the code, out of staff&apos;s reach: the forms&apos; words, the parking prices, the flight search&apos;s three dates, the hero&apos;s temperatures and the board&apos;s column heads. The ‘1 of 3’ on each travel notice is typed too, so adding a notice means renumbering the others.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: a saved change may not reach every traveller straight away.">When staff save a page or a template, Drupal tells Next.js once, after the save, and waits five seconds at most. If that call fails, it is logged and not tried again, and travellers see the old page until the cached copy runs out after an hour. Up to two copies of Next.js run, each with its own cache, and Drupal&apos;s one call reaches only one of them, although I have not tested that. Two tests check that a call without the right secret key is turned away, and none checks a successful save.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: nothing checks a change before it goes live, and some protections are missing.">The automatic checks run on proposed changes, but a change pushed straight to the main branch goes live with no test at all. The site sends only one security header beyond HTTPS, the one that stops other sites showing it inside a frame. The part of Canvas that serves the pages to Next.js is marked experimental, and the Canvas editor does not stop staff placing a component where it does not belong. Drupal&apos;s own address shows a blank page to anyone who opens it, and the home page loads 202 KB of compressed JavaScript in 8 files.</Item>
</List>
</Chapter>

<Chapter label="The lessons" title="Three things I would do the same way again." lede="The airport needed travellers to find flights, notices, waits, parking and the way in on any screen, and its staff to change those answers the same day. These three choices did most of that work, and each one still has a gap I would close.">
<Split>
<List>
  <Item title="Keep the airport's words in its pages, not in the code.">The rulebook the coding agents worked to says the airport&apos;s words live in the pages, and a component holds only the text every page shares, such as the skip link. That is why staff can change a flight row, a security wait or a page&apos;s words in the Canvas editor, across the 29 pages and the 999 components placed on them. The forms broke the rule: their words, the parking prices and the flight search&apos;s three dates are in the code, as one of the limits above says. Next time each of them would get a setting of its own.</Item>
  <Item title="Tell the public site as soon as staff save.">A small piece of code I added to Drupal waits until a saved page or template is stored, then calls Next.js, which draws the pages again. On a test copy of the site, run on my own computer, the first page fetched after a save showed the new title. If the call is missed, each cached page still runs out after an hour. It still needs a cache that both copies of Next.js share, and a test that a save goes through.</Item>
  <Item title="Rehearse a fresh install on every proposed change.">Every proposed change builds the live versions of both services, installs them on an empty database and opens all 29 pages at a desktop and a phone width, checking each one for accessibility. Those steps had passed in the checks and on a test copy of the site, run on my own computer, when the first install on the live service stopped. Drupal&apos;s cache did not yet know one of its modules, so the content recipe, the file that puts the 29 pages in once, could not run. A cache rebuild between the install and the recipe fixed it, and the rehearsal runs that step too. I would also run the same checks before any change goes live.</Item>
</List>
