<Chapter label="Strategy" title="Show clients that Parley has worked in their region, so they write or call." lede="Parley says it is small on purpose. It takes on a limited number of clients and causes, and the person who pitches to a client runs their campaign. It promises that every conversation is confidential, and its team has worked in more than 30 countries. So the site has two jobs. It has to show a client that Parley has worked in their part of the world and who would lead the work, and it has to stay easy for Parley's own staff to keep up to date." split>

<Steps>
  <Step open name="The aim" hint="clients get in touch">Winning means someone who needs Parley's help, or the law firm advising them, sees that Parley has worked in their part of the world, sees who would lead the work, and gets in touch. Both ways in are on the Contact page: a form for a first message, and a 24-hour line for anyone facing a crisis right now. Parley promises that a partner will reply to a message within one working day.</Step>
  <Step name="Where to play" hint="five regions, seven services">Parley works across five regions, Africa, Asia, Europe, Latin America and the Middle East, and offers seven services, from media relations and crisis work to podcasts and film. Its clients need to raise an issue that matters, build a serious voice in their field or answer misleading claims about them, and many reach Parley through international law firms and other agencies. It also says who it is not for. ‘If you want to be on breakfast television tomorrow to launch a new product, we're probably not your agency.’</Step>
  <Step name="How to win" hint="the work shown by place">Parley says most agencies are strong in one of three things, media relations, digital campaigns or international public affairs, and that it does all three together. So the site shows its work by place and names the people who do it. The home page and the Our Work page head each case study with its region and the map position of a city there, such as 4.71° N · 74.07° W for Bogotá. The Team page gives each person their role, such as Partner, Public Affairs & Africa, so a client from West Africa can see who would lead their case.</Step>
  <Step name="What it takes" hint="staff who keep it current">Case studies, services, people and the 24-hour number change, and every page that shows them has to agree. One case study appears on the home page, the Our Work page and its own page, and one service is named on the case studies that use it as well as on the Services page and the home page. Parley's staff have to keep all of that current without a developer. And a first message has to stay private, as Parley has promised.</Step>
  <Step name="How we'd know" hint="enquiries, and who sends them">Success would show in enquiries. Each one is saved with what the client needs help with, one of the seven services or Not sure yet, and who referred them, from a law firm to the press. Over time those two answers would tell Parley which services clients ask for most, and which law firms, agencies and other referrers send them, so it could see where its next clients come from. Parley is imaginary, so there are no real enquiries and no figures.</Step>
</Steps>

<Chapter label="The build" title="Staff edit the content, a developer ships the code, and each message stays private." lede="Enquiries are how Parley would judge the site, and each one has to stay private. Staff change content in WordPress, and a developer changes code only through a deploy, a fresh build of the site that replaces the live one.">

<Chapter label="The limits" title="Where the strategy is not proven yet." lede="Two of the site's rules are built into its code: every case study is filed under a region, and every enquiry is kept inside Parley's own WordPress. Each gap below is set against the part of the strategy it puts at risk.">
<List>
  <Item title="The aim: nobody is told when an enquiry arrives.">Nothing in the site&apos;s code sends an email, so a message waits in WordPress until a member of staff opens the list of enquiries. Until then the client hears nothing from Parley.</Item>
  <Item title="The aim: a message the site turns away gives no reason.">The browser asks for a name, an email address and a message before it sends the form. If a message still reaches the site without one of them, the visitor is taken back to an empty form with no explanation, and what they typed is lost. The consent tick works the other way round: the browser asks for it, but a message sent without it is still saved.</Item>
  <Item title="How to win: the home page shows four of the five regions.">The case-study carousel on the home page is set to show four, in the order staff choose. Asia, fifth in that order, appears only on the Our Work page, so a client from South East Asia who stays on the home page does not see that Parley has worked in their region.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: the enquiry form's topics do not follow a renamed service.">The ‘What can we help with?’ list on the enquiry form is typed into the form, and again into the enquiry record, instead of being read from the services. When a service was renamed on a test copy of the site, run on my own computer, its new name appeared everywhere else the service is shown, but not in that list. Adding a service to it, or changing it, needs a developer.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: the starting content comes back on every deploy.">A script called the seed fills the site with its starting content, and it runs on every deploy. It puts back the title, fields, order and picture of every page and record it created, and every value on the Site settings screen, the 24-hour number included, which suits a demonstration. It leaves alone whatever staff add, the items in the menus and the enquiries. On a live site it would run once, at launch.</Item>
  <Item title="The aim: accessibility has been checked in Chrome only.">Keyboard use, the phone menu, text contrast and the pages without JavaScript were checked by hand and with scripts, in Chrome and its phone mode. The site has not been tested with a screen reader, in Safari or Firefox, or on a real phone, and no automatic accessibility check runs when the site is updated. The thank-you that replaces the form after a message is sent is not announced to a screen reader, so someone using one may not know the message went.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: hardening is unfinished.">The site holds visitors&apos; messages, but it sends no content security policy, a header that tells the browser which scripts to trust, and it still announces its WordPress and PHP versions. Junk messages are stopped only by a security token and a hidden field that people never see but automated programs fill in, and nothing limits how often the form can be sent. Every page also loads its two typefaces from Google, so each visit reaches a company outside the site.</Item>
  <Item title="How we'd know: there are no real enquiries to count.">Each enquiry is saved with what the client needs help with and who referred them, but Parley is imaginary and the form asks visitors not to enter real personal information, so there are none to count. The newsletter box in every footer, The Parley Brief, keeps nothing either, so interest in Parley&apos;s writing cannot be counted.</Item>
</List>
</Chapter>

<Chapter label="The lessons" title="Three things I would do the same way again." lede="Looking back, the goal was to show clients where Parley has worked and to hear from them, on a site its own staff keep current, with every first message kept private. These are the choices that did most of that work.">
<Split>
<List>
  <Item title="Start a case study with a place.">Parley&apos;s work is shown by place, so in the content model a case study starts with one: a region chosen from five, its coordinates and a client line. Staff fill them in on the same form as the rest of the study, and the home page and the Our Work page print them above each case study they show, as the Colombia case study&apos;s form and its three pages show further up this study.</Item>
  <Item title="Choose from a list instead of typing a name twice.">Because a case study chooses its services from the list of services, renaming Media Relations on a test copy of the site changed the name on the four case studies that use it, on the Services page, on the home page and on the service&apos;s own page. The one place it did not reach was the topic list on the enquiry form, which is typed by hand, and that is one of the limits above.</Item>
  <Item title="Keep the first message inside the site.">Confidentiality is part of what Parley offers, so the enquiry form sends a message to Parley&apos;s own WordPress and to no outside service. It is saved as a private record that only signed-in staff can read, with what the client needs help with and who referred them. What it still needs is a notice to staff when a message arrives, which is the first limit above.</Item>
</List>
