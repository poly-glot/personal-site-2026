<Chapter label="Strategy" title="Help professionals choose a certificate, with a site Academy's own staff can run." lede="Academy is an imaginary business-education body in Birmingham. It earns its living from certificates, courses and membership, and its team of 23 keeps the site up to date without a developer. So the site has two jobs: help professionals choose and join, and stay easy for that team to run." split>
<Split reverse>
<Steps>
  <Step open name="The aim" hint="professionals choose and join">Winning means a professional finds the certificate that fits their work, sees what it costs and how it is assessed, and gets in touch to enrol or joins as a member. Both the home and About pages lead with Explore certification, and Join now sits at the top of every page.</Step>
  <Step name="Where to play" hint="certification first">The site is for working professionals and the organisations that train them. Of everything Academy offers, certification comes first: each of the five certificates has a page of its own, while courses, membership and community have one page each. The site does not sell online. Buy this certificate leads to an enquiry.</Step>
  <Step name="How to win" hint="the same facts for every certificate">People compare qualifications before they spend money and time on one. So every certificate page answers the same questions in the same order: level, duration, assessment and price, then who it is for, what it covers and who teaches it. Nobody has to look for the price on one page and the assessment on another.</Step>
  <Step name="What it takes" hint="staff who can edit without a developer">Prices, dates, people and news change, and Academy's staff have to change them without a developer. So each certificate, person and article is a form they fill in, while the layout, colours and contrast are fixed in code. That keeps every page accessible and quick, whoever does the editing.</Step>
  <Step name="How we'd know" hint="enquiries and new members">Success would show in enquiries and new members, so the enquiry form and Join now are the two things to measure. In this demonstration neither one sends anything, so there are no figures. What can be checked today: all five certificates show the same facts, the lowest text contrast on the site is 4.61 to 1, and a new certificate is one form to fill in.</Step>
</Steps>

<Chapter label="Components" title="24 components keep every page consistent, whatever staff add." lede="The site map says what each page holds. Components decide how it looks, once for all 26 pages. A component is a repeated piece of a page, such as the header or a news card, and every page is an arrangement of the 24. Their colours and sizes come from one shared list, called design tokens. So a visitor meets the same patterns everywhere, staff only ever add words and pictures, and a new page is put together from parts that already exist.">

<Chapter label="The limits" title="Where the strategy is not proven yet." lede="The strategy measures success in enquiries and new members. Two of its rules are enforced by the build: code reaches the server only as a new image, and the forms offer staff no formatting. Each gap below is set against the part of the strategy it puts at risk.">
<List>
  <Item title="How we'd know: there are no figures yet.">Join now, Buy this certificate and the contact form end at a message saying this is a demonstration. That is deliberate, so the site collects no spam or personal data, and it means no enquiry or new member can be counted.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: the starting content comes back on every deploy.">A script called the seed fills the site on every deploy, which suits a demonstration. On a live site it would undo the staff&apos;s edits, so it would run once, at launch.</Item>
  <Item title="What it takes: nothing keeps the two sets of stylesheets together.">The static pages and WordPress match today, but no step in the pipeline compares them, so a careless change could separate them unnoticed.</Item>
  <Item title="The aim: accessibility has no automatic test.">Lighthouse and my own contrast checks pass, but no tool checks a page before a deploy. The site aims for level AA of WCAG 2.2. It has not yet been tested with screen readers or with people who have access needs, and its accessibility statement says so.</Item>
  <Item title="A secure site, as the brief asked: hardening is unfinished.">The site sends no content security policy, a header that tells the browser which scripts to trust, and WordPress still announces its version.</Item>
</List>

<Chapter label="The lessons" title="Three things I would do the same way again." lede="The strategy set out to help professionals choose a certificate, on a site Academy&apos;s own staff can run. These are the choices that made that work.">
<Split>
<List>
  <Item title="Start from the organisation's aim.">Putting certification first, with the same facts on every certificate, decided the site map, the components and the forms. The build followed the strategy, not the other way round.</Item>
  <Item title="Decide what staff change before WordPress.">The forms came from the finished pages, so staff got a box for each thing that changes and nothing that can break the design.</Item>
  <Item title="Settle the design while it is cheap to change.">All 26 pages were reviewed as plain web pages first, where a change meant editing one file. WordPress came last.</Item>
</List>
