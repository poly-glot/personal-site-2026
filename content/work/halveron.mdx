<Chapter label="The prototype" title="Every prototype address works on the live site." lede="Those 56 pages existed as plain HTML and CSS before I wrote any WordPress, with the words and images in place. The prototype stays in the repository, and the WordPress theme had to reproduce it. The first check was simple: each prototype page should open at the same address on the live site.">
<Facts>
  <Fact k="Order">The git history shows it. Seven commits built the static site, from the design system to the 13 articles, two rounds of fixes and the image set. The platform, the content model, the seed and the theme came after.</Fact>
  <Fact k="Stylesheets">The theme holds the prototype's 39 component stylesheets unchanged. A file comparison of the two folders finds no difference.</Fact>
  <Fact k="Templates">The theme builds its pages from 53 template parts, small PHP files for pieces such as the enquiry form and the contact box.</Fact>
  <Fact k="Two fixes">Articles live under `/thinking/`, and WordPress would have put that prefix in front of the capability, course, event and sector addresses too. One commit turned it off. Another sent `/search/` to WordPress's own search, because no page existed at that address.</Fact>
  <Fact k="The check">On 15 November 2026 a loop over all 56 prototype addresses got 200 from the live site for each one, the code a server sends for a page that loaded. The loop is a command-line check, not a test in the repository.</Fact>
</Facts>
</Chapter>

<Chapter label="The content model" title="Eight record types that point at each other." lede="Matching addresses proves the pages exist. Staff also had to change them without a developer, so the theme holds the layout and the copy lives in fields described in YAML, a plain text format. A field can also point at another record instead of repeating it.">
<Facts>
  <Fact k="Record types">16 YAML files, 1,314 lines in all, describe 8 types: capability, course, enquiry, event, job, organisation, person and sector. Articles use WordPress's own posts in four kinds: news, in brief, white paper and case study.</Fact>
  <Fact k="Edit screens">The yacf plugin, part of the WordPress image shared across the agency's client sites, reads the YAML and builds the edit screens in wp-admin, WordPress's staff area.</Fact>
  <Fact k="Links">27 fields point from one record to another. A sector names its contact, its client logos and its case studies. An article names its author, capabilities and sectors. A forum meeting names the organisation that hosts it.</Fact>
  <Fact k="One person">Ruth Calloway is one person record. The Business transformation and Utilities pages name her as their contact, two articles name her as author and the careers page quotes her. The Talk to Ruth box takes her name, email and phone from that record.</Fact>
  <Fact k="Organisations">22 invented organisations with their own logos fill four roles: 8 clients, 6 forum members, 4 accrediting bodies and 4 technology partners.</Fact>
  <Fact k="Reading links">The theme loads each list of links in one query, in the order the editor set, and keeps only published records of the right type. If staff unpublish a person, that person drops off every page that named them.</Fact>
  <Fact k="Seed">The seed, a PHP script of 2,696 lines, writes every record into WordPress on each deploy and is safe to rerun. It creates people and organisations first, then the records that point at them, and comes back after the articles to fill in each sector's case studies.</Fact>
</Facts>
</Chapter>

<Chapter label="The enquiry" title="From a sector page to a private enquiry." lede="The enquiry is one of those eight record types, and it is where the operations leader from the first chapter ends up. The contact page, each sector page and each white-paper download carry a version of the same form. All three post to one handler in the theme, with no form plugin.">
<List>
  <Item title="It records where the message came from.">The form sends the id of the page it sits on. The handler accepts only a published page, article or sector, and sends anything else back to the home page.</Item>
  <Item title="A bot gets no sign it was caught.">Each form carries a nonce, a token WordPress issues with the page and checks when the form comes back, and a hidden field called website that people never see. A bot that fills it in gets the usual thank-you, and the handler saves nothing.</Item>
  <Item title="Interests must match a real capability.">The white-paper form asks which capabilities interest you. The handler keeps a choice only if it names a real capability and drops anything invented.</Item>
  <Item title="Each form has its own rules.">A name and a valid email are always required. The contact and sector forms also need a message, and a download needs a company name.</Item>
  <Item title="Saved where only staff can see it.">The handler saves the enquiry as a private record. Staff find it in wp-admin under Enquiries, with columns for the form, the page it was sent from, the company and the email. No email goes out.</Item>
</List>
</Chapter>

<Chapter label="The limits" title="Where it stops today." lede="Halveron is a demonstration that takes no money and no bookings. The clearest gap is on the enquiry form, and the rest come from choices I made in the build.">
<List>
  <Item title="The form note contradicts the handler.">The note under the form says messages are not stored or read, but the handler saves each one as a private enquiry. I would change the note to say what the code does.</Item>
  <Item title="Dates stay where staff left them.">Staff set each course's next date and pick each next forum meeting, and nothing moves them on. On 15 November 2026 the home page still shows a Lean Practitioner course on 10 November. I would have the theme hide a date once it has passed.</Item>
  <Item title="The seed overwrites staff edits.">A change made in wp-admin to a seeded record lasts until the next deploy, when the seed writes its own copy back. For a real client I would seed once and then leave the content to staff.</Item>
  <Item title="No checks run before a deploy.">The repository has no tests, no HTML validator and no accessibility checker, and the deploy pipeline builds the site and ships it. The accessibility statement says screen-reader testing has not yet been completed. I would commit the 56-address check as the first test.</Item>
  <Item title="Nothing sends email.">Enquiries wait in wp-admin and the newsletter box stores nothing. The conference page asks people to book by email, and courses cannot be booked at all.</Item>
</List>
</Chapter>
